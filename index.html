Intro

Improving traffic safety requires a multi-disciplinary approach.

On average, 500 people are seriously injured or killed each year while traveling in Berkeley, Charleston, and Dorchester counties. That's 500 preventable emergency room visits and funerals. We can do better. Through safer, slower street design, proactive enforcement, higher standards for vehicle maintenance, and improved emergency access, we can make getting home safely a matter of when and not if. To get there, this will require real resource investments from the public, their elected leaders, emergency responders, vehicle manufacturers, maintenance crews, traffic engineers, and transportation planners.

Performance

There are on average 72 reported crashes per day in our region.

And that's just the crashes that end up in police crash reports. BCDCOG estimates that for every 1 traffic injury that gets reported to the police, there's an additional 1.4 traffic injuries that don't get reported but result in an emergency room visit. Overall, traffic injuries are the second leading cause of emergency room visits within the region, accounting for 23% of all visits (second only to "falls" at 28%).

Target

Our region aims to reduce traffic fatalities by 10% between 2026 and 2030.

The number of traffic fatalities has gradually increased in our region, from 569 deaths (2014–2018) to 649 deaths (2019–2023). This 14% increase in traffic fatalities in our region outpaced a 12% increase nationally. But a world with ever-increasing traffic fatalities is not inevitable. Over this same time period, 83 counties across the country were able to improve traffic safety conditions and reduce the number of fatalities on their streets. On average, these counties saw a 10% reduction in traffic fatalities over a 5-year period. A 10% decrease in our region is possible, too, and will serve as our first benchmark on our way to an ultimate target of 0 traffic fatalities.

Excess Speed

Reducing serious and fatal injuries starts with reducing speeds.

Excess speed was the most common contributing factor identified in police crash report narratives, contributing to 25% of serious and fatal injuries. Some street segments experience isolated speeding, with drivers more likely to exceed the posted speed limit on a specific day of the week or hour of the day. While other street segments experience regular, sustained speeding.

Strategies

Because safety needs surpass available funding, projects must be prioritized.

BCDCOG prioritized high-risk corridors through a combination of crash history (weighted by injury severity), excess speed, and street geometric features correlated with serious and fatal injuries. Implementation of the regional safety plan will rely on expanding the number of corridor-specific traffic safety projects and regionwide implementation of safety programming.
